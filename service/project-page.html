note-seq · practice tools

Audio ↔ score sync

Give it a piece of sheet music and a recording of that piece. It works out when each bar of the score is played in that recording — then serves a page where you click any bars you like and hear exactly those.

The problem it solves

A printed score says what to play, not when. It has no clock. A recording has a clock but no names: you can hear two and a half minutes of music without knowing that bar 63 has just gone past. So if you want to practise bars 40–48, you hunt for them by ear, every time.

This service builds the missing table: for one recording of one piece, when does each bar start and end? Think of subtitles for a film — except the “words” are bars of music, and the timings come from listening to the recording rather than from typing them in.

How it does it, without understanding music

  1. Take the two inputsThe score as MusicXML (what notation software exports), and a recording — a file, or a YouTube link.
  2. Read the score as structure, not soundWhich bars exist, what is in each one, and where each bar’s ink sits on the page.
  3. Make a boring, perfect versionA plain computer rendition of the score: mechanical, unmusical, and with timing known to the millisecond.
  4. Compare the two sounds, allowing for the humanThe reference and your recording are matched moment by moment, letting one drift, pause and hurry against the other. That technique is called dynamic time warping; it is the part that makes this work on a real performance.
  5. Turn that into a table, and engrave the scoreEvery bar gets its seconds in your recording; the score is drawn as clean web pages with the position of every bar on them, so a click can be turned back into bars.

What you get

A page showing the piece you uploaded, engraved. Click one bar, then another, and you hear exactly that stretch of the music — not whatever seconds it happens to occupy in some other performance. A playhead follows along and the bar underneath it lights up. There are also downloadable pieces of the result: the bar-to-seconds map, a clip of any range, and the engraved pages.

Measured, rather than claimed

Alignment of a 5-minute Chopin waltz (204 bars)8.6 seconds, from upload to finished page
Click accuracy on a 127-bar pieceevery bar’s clickable area lands within 0.02 px of its printed notes
Clip of bars 1–1843.5 seconds of audio, and byte-identical to the same clip cut on the machine that analysed it
One piece in storageabout 16 MB — nearly all of it the recording itself

What it is not

It does not transcribe audio into notes, and it does not judge how well anyone played. It does not need a studio recording — only a recording of that piece.

A very free performance can still defeat it: long unwritten pauses, big jumps, or a recording that only covers part of the piece. When the result is doubtful it says so rather than guessing quietly — the map carries a confidence for each bar, and warnings for the bars it had to force.

For the curious

The alignment and the engraving are the same code the desktop practice app uses — a shared library, not a reimplementation. Scores are parsed and drawn with Verovio, the sound comparison uses librosa, and the reference performance is rendered with FluidSynth. The web half is FastAPI and React, and it is deliberately small: it needs none of the music-analysis machinery, because the analysis happens on the machine that owns the recording.
